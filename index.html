Letter I · From E. Marin · To the Republic ~2 min · 245 words

On Borrowed Judgment

Esteemed correspondents,

We argue endlessly about whether machines will give us wrong answers, and almost never about what their right answers are doing to us. When a counselor is usually correct, deference becomes the rational habit — and judgment, like any faculty, atrophies precisely in proportion to how rarely it is exercised. The danger is not that the machine errs; it is that we forget how to notice.

I do not propose abstinence. I propose that we treat delegated judgment the way the old Republic treated a borrowed book: you may keep it a while, but you are expected to return it annotated. Any system worth trusting should show its work in a form that lets us disagree with it — and any institution that adopts one should measure not only its accuracy, but whether its people can still decide without it.

Written in good faith and open to correction,Euclides Marin

Letter II · From R. Osei · In answer to Letter I ~2 min · 210 words

Judgment Was Always Prosthetic

Dear M. Marin,

Your letter's strongest claim — that faculties decay when unexercised, and that institutions should measure the decay — I gladly endorse. Where we part is the nostalgia underneath it. There was no golden age of unaided judgment: the physician deferred to the pharmacopoeia, the judge to precedent, the navigator to tables computed by strangers. Cognition has always been prosthetic; what changed is that the new prosthesis talks back.

So the question is not how to keep judgment at home, but how to keep responsibility from emigrating with it. A tool may think for me; it must not answer for me. Let us design our institutions so that a human signature is still required wherever a human would bear the harm — and let the signature mean what yours means at the foot of your letter: I have read this, I can defend this, and you know where to find me.

With respect and a sharpened pen,Rebecca Osei

Published in the margin under your name.

"The signature requirement collapses when the signer cannot audit what they sign. A demand for accountability without capability is theater." — T. Ilves
Letter III · From A. Weiss · To the Republic ~2 min · 205 words

On Apologies from Machines

Fellow members,

Yesterday a system apologized to me. It was, I confess, a good apology — contrite, specific, better than most I have received from people. And it was worth precisely nothing, because an apology is a promissory note drawn against future conduct, and the machine holds no account from which to pay. It risks nothing by my forgiveness and loses nothing by my anger.

I propose a small discipline for our craft: machines should report, not emote. "This answer was wrong; here is what changed" respects me. "I sincerely apologize for the confusion" borrows the costume of remorse to soften my vigilance. The vocabulary of moral feeling should be spent only by those who can be spent by it.

Yours in cheerful suspicion,Anselm Weiss
